A guide from Cigar Locker

How to organize a cigar collection.

A collection is organized when you can answer three questions without opening a humidor: what do I have, where is it, and how long has it been there. This guide is a method that works on paper, in a spreadsheet or in an app.

1. Record purchases, not single cigars

The useful unit is the purchase: a box, a bundle or a handful of singles bought together. Give each purchase one line. Two boxes of the same cigar bought in different years are two lines, because their age and price differ.

2. Write these down the day you buy

WhatWhy it earns its place
Maker, line and sizeThe size by name and by figures (6 x 52). Many lines sell one band in several sizes.
WrapperLines sold in Natural and Maduro share a band. The wrapper is what tells them apart later.
How manyAnd whether it was a box, a bundle or singles.
Date boughtResting time is counted from here. A guessed date is better marked unknown.
What you paidFor the whole purchase. It is the only cost figure that is a fact.
Where you bought itYou will want to find the shop again.
Which humidorOne name per humidor, the same every time.

Write the size as the two numbers on the box, length in inches by ring gauge, as well as its name. Size names are not standard: one maker’s Toro is 6 by 50 and another’s is 6 by 54. These are the sizes most often meant, to help you read an old note:

NameTypical sizeWhat to know
Petit corona4½ × 42A short, slim cigar.
Corona5½ × 42A classic size; many older blends were made for it.
Robusto5 × 50Short and thick; nearly every maker offers one.
Toro6 × 50 to 54Varies most from maker to maker.
Churchill7 × 47 to 50Long, and thinner than a Toro of today.
Lancero7 to 7½ × 38 to 40Long and slender, often finished with a pigtail cap.
Gordo6 × 60Also sold as a Gigante or a 60.
Belicoso or Torpedo5½ to 6½ × 52A pointed head; the ring gauge is taken at the widest part.

Ring gauge counts 64ths of an inch, so a 64 ring cigar would be a full inch across.

3. Name your humidors, then your shelves

Give each humidor a name that says where it is. If a cabinet is large, name its shelves or drawers and keep like with like: by maker, or by how ready the cigars are. The scheme matters less than using only one.

4. Use photographs

  • Photograph the band, close and square on. It settles later arguments about which cigar it was.
  • Photograph the box’s end label. It usually prints the size, the wrapper and the count.
  • Photograph a shelf after you tidy it. It is a quick check of what was where.

5. Record what leaves

A count goes wrong when cigars leave unrecorded. Note each one that goes, and how: enjoyed, given away, traded, lost or damaged. Keep gifts apart from the cigars you enjoyed yourself, or your own history stops being yours.

6. Keep notes on the ones you enjoy

A rating and a few words are enough: what you tasted, how it burned, whether you would buy it again. Notes written the same evening are worth more than a memory a year later.

7. Audit twice a year

Open each humidor, count each purchase and correct the record. Twice a year is enough for most collections. An audit is also when to move what has rested long enough to the front.

Paper, spreadsheet or app

Paper works for a single small humidor. A spreadsheet works until you want counts that change by themselves, photographs, or a second humidor. An app is worth it when it saves the typing: identifying a cigar from its band, filling in the blend, and keeping the count as cigars come and go.

Questions about organizing a collection

What should I record for each cigar?

For each purchase: the maker, line and size, the wrapper, how many, the date bought, what you paid, where you bought it and which humidor it is in.

How often should I count my collection?

Twice a year is enough for most collections, as long as cigars that leave are recorded when they leave.

Is a spreadsheet enough to keep track of a cigar collection?

For a small collection, yes. It becomes work once you want counts that update themselves, photographs, more than one humidor, or a history of where each cigar went.
